## Abstract

This document proposes the **Federated Execution Operator Ecosystem**: an architectural and economic framework through which independently operated execution environments may participate in a shared ecosystem while preserving consistent governance, interoperability, accountability, and execution evidence.

The model extends beyond conventional centralized Software-as-a-Service architectures.

Rather than requiring a single platform provider to own and operate every execution environment, customer relationship, market channel, integration, and domain-specific capability, the proposed ecosystem enables independent participants to:

* deploy and operate governed execution infrastructure;
* serve local, regional, industry-specific, or specialized markets;
* build processing capabilities and platform extensions;
* create integrations and domain-specific execution solutions;
* support customer adoption;
* educate users and develop communities;
* contribute execution knowledge;
* participate economically in the value they help create.

The model introduces the possibility that a **Deployment Unit** may serve simultaneously as:

* a technical boundary;
* a governance boundary;
* an operational boundary;
* a trust boundary;
* a data-sovereignty boundary;
* and an economic participation boundary.

CaralisLabs may act as a platform steward by maintaining common architecture, governance mechanisms, standards, reference implementations, compatibility requirements, shared services, research, and continued product development without being required to centrally operate every execution environment.

The framework is based on a foundational principle:

> **Value should follow attributable contribution.**

This RFC defines architectural and economic principles. It does not establish a franchise system, commercial partnership, agency relationship, compensation entitlement, revenue-sharing formula, operator authorization, or binding commercial program.

## 1. Motivation

Artificial intelligence is increasingly moving beyond content generation and advisory assistance toward the execution of consequential work.

AI systems may:

* invoke tools;
* coordinate agents;
* execute workflows;
* transform enterprise information;
* interact with operational systems;
* produce regulated artifacts;
* support professional decisions;
* initiate actions;
* and participate in increasingly complex economic activity.

As execution becomes more capable, the infrastructure supporting it must address:

* authority;
* policy;
* accountability;
* evidence;
* provenance;
* interoperability;
* operational responsibility;
* and value attribution.

At the same time, centralized platform models may create structural limitations.

A single platform provider may be expected to:

* operate all infrastructure;
* enter every geographic market;
* understand every regulated domain;
* establish every customer relationship;
* develop every specialized capability;
* support every integration;
* provide all implementation services;
* and finance all continued research and development.

Such centralization may limit ecosystem growth and constrain the ability of local operators, domain specialists, extension developers, integrators, educators, and adoption partners to create independent economic activity around execution infrastructure.

The Execution Economy requires more than scalable execution.

It may also require scalable participation.

This RFC proposes a federated model in which common execution architecture and governance mechanisms may coexist with independent operation, local specialization, distributed value creation, and future economic participation.

## 2. Core Thesis

> **The Execution Economy should enable participants not only to consume AI execution, but also to build capabilities, operate execution infrastructure, create adoption, serve markets, and participate economically in the value they help create.**

The proposed model separates:

* common execution infrastructure from local operation;
* platform stewardship from customer ownership;
* architectural compatibility from centralized control;
* ecosystem participation from platform employment;
* contribution from automatic entitlement;
* and public architectural principles from proprietary commercial implementations.

## 3. From Centralized SaaS to Federated Execution

### 3.1 Conventional SaaS Model

A conventional Software-as-a-Service model commonly centralizes:

* infrastructure ownership;
* platform operation;
* application delivery;
* customer access;
* billing;
* product development;
* data processing;
* support;
* and commercial relationships.

The provider typically operates the service, and customers consume functionality through centrally managed interfaces.

This model offers important benefits, including:

* operational consistency;
* simplified upgrades;
* centralized security management;
* unified customer experience;
* and predictable service delivery.

However, centralized SaaS may also create limitations when execution environments require:

* local infrastructure ownership;
* regional operation;
* data residency;
* customer-controlled deployment;
* industry-specific governance;
* specialized domain capabilities;
* sovereign operation;
* independent commercial relationships;
* or ecosystem-driven expansion.

---

### 3.2 Federated Execution Model

A Federated Execution Operator Ecosystem MAY distribute operational and commercial participation while preserving common execution principles.

Independent participants MAY:

* operate approved or compatible Deployment Units;
* serve customers directly;
* develop specialized capabilities;
* integrate governed execution into existing platforms;
* provide implementation and support services;
* develop regional or industry-specific offerings;
* and participate in future ecosystem economic mechanisms.

The platform steward MAY provide:

* core execution architecture;
* governance mechanisms;
* reference implementations;
* deployment lifecycle tooling;
* interoperability specifications;
* compatibility requirements;
* shared registries;
* trusted capability models;
* execution evidence mechanisms;
* ecosystem standards;
* certification programs;
* common services;
* research;
* and continued platform development.

The model does not require every Deployment Unit to be centrally hosted or directly operated by the platform steward.

## 4. Core Principle

> **Execution infrastructure MAY be operated independently while governance, interoperability, execution evidence, and ecosystem compatibility remain consistently defined.**

Independent operation MUST NOT imply the absence of governance.

Federation MUST NOT imply unrestricted execution.

Operator autonomy SHOULD coexist with:

* explicit authority;
* bounded capabilities;
* policy enforcement;
* execution traceability;
* evidence preservation;
* version compatibility;
* security requirements;
* and clearly assigned operational responsibility.

## 5. Deployment Units as Multi-Dimensional Boundaries

Within the proposed ecosystem, a Deployment Unit MAY represent multiple boundaries simultaneously.

A Deployment Unit MAY define:

* compute resources;
* storage resources;
* networking;
* execution services;
* processing elements;
* platform services;
* deployment topology;
* service versions;
* and runtime dependencies.

---

### 5.2 Governance Boundary

A Deployment Unit MAY define:

* execution policies;
* authority scopes;
* admissibility requirements;
* approval requirements;
* capability permissions;
* identity relationships;
* tenant boundaries;
* evidence requirements;
* and governance responsibilities.

---

### 5.3 Operational Boundary

A Deployment Unit MAY have an identifiable operator responsible for:

* deployment;
* configuration;
* availability;
* upgrades;
* monitoring;
* incident response;
* backup;
* recovery;
* customer support;
* and operational compliance.

### 5.4 Trust Boundary

A Deployment Unit MAY establish:

* trusted identities;
* trusted processing elements;
* approved integrations;
* authenticated execution contexts;
* signed execution evidence;
* and explicit trust relationships with other systems.

---

### 5.5 Data-Sovereignty Boundary

A Deployment Unit MAY support:

* regional data residency;
* customer-controlled storage;
* local processing;
* regulated data handling;
* infrastructure sovereignty;
* and jurisdiction-specific requirements.

---

### 5.6 Economic Participation Boundary

A Deployment Unit MAY become an identifiable unit through which:

* infrastructure costs are incurred;
* execution services are delivered;
* customers are served;
* capabilities are consumed;
* operators create value;
* contributors participate;
* economic activity is measured;
* and future value-sharing mechanisms are applied.

## 10. Value Attribution

Value attribution is the process of identifying contributions associated with execution, adoption, operation, capability creation, or measurable ecosystem outcomes.

Attribution MAY consider:

* capability participation;
* execution participation;
* operator responsibility;
* pipeline contribution;
* extension use;
* customer introduction;
* implementation activity;
* adoption outcomes;
* execution evidence;
* service delivery;
* and contractual relationships.

Attribution MUST NOT be assumed to be equivalent to compensation.

Attribution MAY provide evidence for future economic mechanisms, but compensation requires explicit rules, agreements, programs, or contracts.

## 11. Execution Evidence as an Attribution Foundation

Governed execution may provide evidence regarding:

* which capabilities participated;
* which versions executed;
* which operator environment hosted execution;
* which policies were applied;
* which artifacts were produced;
* which approvals occurred;
* which execution steps completed;
* which outcomes were observed;
* and which contributors or services were associated with execution.

Such evidence MAY support future attribution systems.

However:

* execution participation does not necessarily establish causal economic contribution;
* technical invocation does not automatically establish ownership;
* attribution does not automatically create payment rights;
* and observed correlation does not necessarily establish value creation.

Future attribution mechanisms SHOULD distinguish:

* execution evidence;
* causal contribution;
* contractual entitlement;
* commercial participation;
* and economic settlement.

## 40. Conclusion

The Execution Economy is not only concerned with systems performing work.

It is also concerned with:

* who builds execution capabilities;
* who operates execution infrastructure;
* who creates adoption;
* who serves customers and communities;
* who contributes knowledge;
* how contribution becomes visible;
* and how value may be shared among those who help create it.

Centralized SaaS is not the only possible model for delivering AI execution infrastructure.

A federated ecosystem may allow independent operators, capability developers, domain specialists, integrators, educators, adoption partners, and customers to participate while common governance, interoperability, trust, and execution evidence remain consistently defined.

Deployment Units may become more than deployment targets.

They may become independently operable units through which governed execution is delivered, local businesses are created, specialized markets are served, and economic participation becomes possible.

CaralisLabs does not need to operate every execution environment in order to provide the architecture, standards, governance mechanisms, shared infrastructure, research, and continued development that allow an ecosystem of independently operated Deployment Units to emerge.

The long-term objective is not merely to distribute software.

It is to enable a governed execution ecosystem in which participants can:

> **Build capabilities. Operate infrastructure. Create adoption. Serve markets. Participate in value.**
